The road to Mainpat winds gently upwards through forests and rolling hills until, almost without warning, the air begins to change. Cooler. Quieter. Lighter.

People often call Mainpat the "Shimla of Chhattisgarh." I now understand why.

For two days, this small plateau became home.

Not in a resort or a city hotel, but at an ex-Army officer's farmhouse — a modest homestay surrounded by pear trees, litchis, mangoes and countless other fruit trees. The owner had traded military life for farming, and somehow both identities were visible in the place. The discipline remained; only the landscape had changed.

Across the road stood the Sangwari training centre. That was where the real journey began.

Unlike my previous visit to Jan Swasthya Sahyog, where I was witnessing a model that had evolved over decades, Sangwari felt different.

It felt young. Still under construction. Still experimenting. Still learning. And strangely, that made it even more fascinating.

Ashwini and Nisha became my guides over the next two days.

There were no polished presentations waiting for us. Instead, there were conversations. Lots of them.

Conversations about children. Nutrition. Apps. Government systems. Village workers. Migration. Trust.

And the thousand tiny decisions that determine whether healthcare actually reaches a family.

## Finding the Child Before the Illness Does

One sentence stayed with me throughout the visit.

> "The real work is to find the child before the illness finds the child."

Everything else seemed to flow from that idea.

Sangwari's work begins long before a child reaches a doctor.

Community workers identify children showing early signs of severe malnutrition. Families are counselled. Nutrition is monitored. Pregnant women are followed up. Vaccination status is checked. When required, children are referred to clinics.

It sounds straightforward when written like this. It isn't.

Because life refuses to stay inside neat program designs.

One of the biggest challenges, I learned, wasn't nutrition. It was *migration*.

Families leave for brick kilns. Children disappear from records for weeks or months. Then they return.

How do you run a nutrition program when your beneficiaries keep moving? How do you ensure continuity when the village itself keeps changing?

The answer wasn't a sophisticated software system. It was *relationships*.

The community workers knew families by name. They knew who had migrated. Who had returned. Whose child had been ill. Who needed another visit.

Healthcare, here, looked remarkably human.

## The Kuniya Clinic

We later visited the Kuniya clinic. From outside, it looked unremarkable. Inside, it functioned like a beautifully choreographed orchestra.

Patients first stopped at registration. Then moved to the doctor. Some went for laboratory investigations. Others walked directly to the pharmacy. One small room handled dressing and minor procedures.

![Registration at the Kuniya clinic](/img/2026-07-08-building-hope-one-small-system-at-a-time/mainpat-1.png)

![Inside the clinic](/img/2026-07-08-building-hope-one-small-system-at-a-time/mainpat-2.png)

Everything was modest. Nothing looked expensive. Yet nothing appeared chaotic either.

It reminded me that efficiency has surprisingly little to do with the size of a building. It has much more to do with clarity of purpose.

The waiting area was full. Most patients belonged to tribal communities. Nobody had asked them to travel here. They came because they trusted the people inside.

Trust, I realised once again, is probably healthcare's most valuable infrastructure.

## A Mud House That Was Not Just a Mud House

Later that day we visited one of Sangwari's nutrition homes. Calling it a "centre" almost feels misleading. It was simply a mud house. Except it wasn't.

Inside lived something much bigger than the building itself. Two didis were caring for six young children.

The walls had been painted using natural colours. The rooms were spotless. Everything had its place. The children were completely at ease. One corner stored food. Another held records.

We tasted the sattu that forms an important part of the children's nutritional support. Simple. Nutritious. Practical.

The didis showed us their records. Every ration distributed. Every medicine given. Every child visited. Every follow-up done.

Once again, I found myself wondering why we continue to believe that "good systems" only exist inside modern offices. Sometimes they exist inside mud houses.

## The Training Session

Perhaps the most inspiring part of the visit happened the following morning.

Ashwini and Nisha were conducting a training session for community health workers on the Avni application.

Some participants had started using the application only a month earlier. Some had barely begun.

I expected hesitation. Instead, I found curiosity.

Questions kept coming.

- "What if the child's name doesn't appear?"
- "Why is this calculation different?"
- "Should this child be marked red or yellow?"

Every question represented someone trying to serve families better.

![A training session for community health workers](/img/2026-07-08-building-hope-one-small-system-at-a-time/mainpat-3.png)

The application itself is still evolving. A few issues surfaced that had escaped earlier testing. Nobody complained. Screenshots were taken. Feedback was noted. Workarounds were discussed.

![The Avni home dashboard, in Hindi](/img/2026-07-08-building-hope-one-small-system-at-a-time/mainpat-4.png)

![Screening figures for children under three](/img/2026-07-08-building-hope-one-small-system-at-a-time/mainpat-5.png)

Everyone seemed remarkably comfortable with one simple truth. The software was still work-in-progress.

And that was okay. Because improving it was everyone's responsibility. That mindset impressed me far more than the application itself.

One discussion during the training stayed with me.

The trainers repeatedly reminded everyone not to become obsessed with the app.

First understand the child. Then understand the nutrition. Then understand the program. Only then worry about the data.

That felt like an important lesson for anyone working in digital health. Technology should never become the centre of the conversation. People should.

## The Systems We Do Not Measure

As the visit progressed, another thought quietly emerged.

We often judge organisations by outcomes. Number of beneficiaries. Projects completed. Awards received. Funding raised.

But perhaps we should pay more attention to something else.

Their systems. Not the software systems. Human systems.

How people are trained. How knowledge is shared. How new workers are supported. How mistakes are discussed. How trust is built. How communities participate.

These invisible systems determine whether visible outcomes last.

Throughout the visit, Sangwari reminded me of something I had been reflecting on since [Anuppur](/blog/2026-07-04-stories-that-never-reach-us/).

Healthcare is rarely transformed by one big breakthrough. It changes because thousands of small systems begin working together.

A village worker identifies a child early. A nutrition didi prepares another meal. A nurse follows up. A doctor diagnoses on time. Someone updates a register. Someone fixes a bug in an app. Someone makes another home visit.

Individually, these actions seem insignificant. Together, they become healthcare.

## Where Journeys Begin

On my final evening in Mainpat, I walked back towards the homestay. The orchards were quiet. The training centre across the road had fallen silent after a long day.

I found myself thinking about how different this visit had been from [Anuppur](/blog/2026-07-04-stories-that-never-reach-us/). JSS showed me what decades of patient institution-building can create.

Sangwari showed me something equally important. How those journeys begin.

With rented buildings. Temporary clinics. Dedicated community workers. Evolving software application. Insightful feedback sessions.

And an unwavering belief that every small improvement matters.

Perhaps that is how meaningful institutions are built. Not through grand plans.

But through thousands of ordinary days where people simply decide to return to work tomorrow and make one small thing better than it was yesterday.
